# Chapter 995

Some conversations don’t need many words.

Especially when the matter is urgent.

That was true of my conversation with Jin Wikyung, who’d come to my room out of the blue. It lasted less than fifteen minutes.

But what he told me weighed as heavily as a massive boulder. As soon as he left, I called everyone together—the people who could help me bear that weight.

Rattle.

“Well, I’ll be damned. Live long enough and you see everything. Who’d have thought martial-world lowlifes like us would ever lay eyes on the Marquis of Shangshan?”

Namho tossed out the silly joke the moment the door opened, and I couldn’t help laughing.

For two reasons.

First, seeing Namho again—or rather, seeing all of them—felt good. Comfortable, too, as though it had been ages since we’d met.

Second, Namho was perched on Taishan’s shoulders as though that were the most natural place in the world to sit.

“What are you doing?”

“What does it look like? Sitting.”

“I can see that. Why are you sitting *there*?”

“Easy. It’s incredibly comfortable. This fellow’s a real find.”

Namho smiled with the satisfaction of an inventor who’d made a world-changing discovery, then patted the top of Taishan’s head.

“What are you waiting for? Get inside.”

Taishan stood blankly in the doorway. He glanced off into the distance before answering.

“Taishan. Feet won’t move. Must be hungry.”

“What the hell are you talking about? The jerky you stuffed into your mouth barely fifteen minutes ago amounted to a whole calf.”

“……”

Apparently, the ride was pay-as-you-go.

And the mileage was terrible.

“Don’t remember. Hungry.”

“You bottomless pit…”

With a deep sigh, Namho pulled a piece of taffy from his sleeve and waved it in front of Taishan’s eyes.

“There. Will you move now?”

Taishan’s eyes narrowed sharply.

“Just one?”

“Damn it. I’ll give you another when we get back.”

“Three.”

“You little bandit. Not a chance. Two. That’s my final offer, so think carefully.”

Taishan’s resolve was beginning to waver when a voice sounded from behind his enormous body, ending the negotiations.

“Move. Now. Or I’ll personally stab each of you twice.”

“……!”

“……!”

“What, two stabs each aren’t enough? Should I make it three?”

Her voice was low and calm, which somehow made the warning more dangerous. Taishan swallowed hard and hurried into the room.

He still remembered to snatch the taffy Namho had been waving in his face.

With the doorway finally clear, Ju Hwaran—the one who’d put an end to the rush-hour jam—strode toward me. She called out in a voice suddenly thick with emotion, as though she hadn’t just threatened to stab anyone.

“Young Master Jin! No, Benefactor! No, Pavilion Master!”

“……”

I was pretty sure I’d asked her several times to pick one.

It was funny, but her sudden change of attitude caught me off guard. While I floundered, her eyes filled with tears.

“I’m sorry I arrived so late during the last battle. You must’ve had such a hard time.”

Behind Ju Hwaran, Sama Pyo stood like an invisible man and muttered to himself.

“I’d guess the enemies had a harder time.”

Song Ilseom, arms crossed over the willow-leaf saber tucked against his chest as always, nodded.

“Judging by the aftermath, he was a monster. The enemies were smashed to pieces.”

Ju Hwaran clenched her jaw and ignored them.

“Were you hurt? Are you all right now?”

Sama Pyo and Song Ilseom at least had some sense. They slipped away at the right moment instead of barging in like Hyuk Mujin.

“Come on, Young Lady Ju. Why ask how someone’s feeling when he went through Bone Transformation just the other day—”

A chill settled over the room.

I couldn’t see Ju Hwaran’s expression as she slowly turned, but I saw the terror spread across Hyuk Mujin’s face as his voice trailed off. I decided I could live the rest of my life without finding out what she looked like.

“Mujin, shut your blabbering mouth. Young Lady Ju, I’m fine. Completely fine, so please don’t worry. And Taishan, I have something important to say, so swallow that taffy before we start—oh, you’ve finished it already. Did you chew it, or drink it?”

With Taishan’s magic trick over—and me wondering whether taffy might actually be a liquid—I’d finally sorted out the traffic. Namho gave me an uneasy look.

“Something important, huh? Why do I already have a bad feeling about this? Am I just worrying for nothing?”

“If only that were all.”

I wasn’t the one who said it.

The last guest, Jeok Cheongang, strode into the room and gave me a nod.

“Tell them what’s happening beyond Shanxi Province.”

His grave eyes told me he’d heard the same news I had.

I looked silently from one face to the next in the now-crowded room. At last, I spoke.

“Something troubling is happening in the west.”

“The west? Where in the west…?”

As expected, Namho was the first to react. His brow furrowed as he spoke, then his narrow eyes widened.

“Y-you don’t mean…?”

“Yes. The place you’re thinking of.”

I nodded and named the accursed land at the far western edge—the birthplace of the Heavenly Demon Divine Cult, called the Demonic Cult in the Central Plains, and now Dark Heaven’s base.

“Xinjiang.”

“……!”

“……!”

Shock swept through the room like an invisible wave.

* * *

When did Murim—that other world within the world—and the martial artists who lived in it first come into being?

No one knew for sure.

Oral traditions passed down through the generations, along with the few surviving lines in records from the distant past, led many to claim that Shaolin Temple in Henan had begun Murim’s long history. In time, that became the accepted account.

Jeok Cheongang had told me something different while I was enduring grueling training on Mount Jiuhua.

*“History is shaped and pieced together. People believe what they want to believe—whatever suits them.”*

Back then, Jeok Cheongang had been suffering from the infirmities of old age, and he’d begun reminiscing more often. Or rather, I’d half-begged him to. I thought I’d read somewhere that recalling and revisiting old memories whenever he had time might slow the illness’s progression.

And among Jeok Cheongang’s vast store of knowledge were secret histories of Murim that few people knew.

*“Light and darkness go hand in hand. The idea that martial arts were born in Shaolin, packed with fellows trying to become Buddhas, is absurd enough. But did you think no one would try to use such power for evil?”*

*“Are you saying…?”*

*“That’s right. According to records left by our sect’s forebears, the Demonic Cult came into being that way, too. No one knows exactly when, but it was probably around the same time as Shaolin Temple.”*

Murim could only have become a mountain of sabers and a forest of swords because the age itself was so chaotic.

The Shaolin warrior monks learned martial arts to protect. Others, on encountering that astonishing power, learned martial arts to conquer the world.

*“People of every stripe must’ve come flocking from all directions. Someone used martial arts to wield power so uncanny he hardly seemed human. Imagine how astonished they must’ve been.”*

There must have been only a few dozen of them at first.

The world around them was dark even in daylight. Having encountered a power like a great light, they soon became followers and spread in every direction.

They told others what they’d seen with their own eyes. When people refused to believe them, they took hold of their sleeves and led them to him.

To the man with that unbelievable power. To the leader who would protect them in that wretched age of chaos.

The followers grew to hundreds, then thousands, and finally a hundred thousand. Their leader declared himself the Cult Leader and a divine man sent by Heaven.

*“That was the beginning of the Heavenly Demon and the Demonic Cult.”*

Jeok Cheongang had added that they probably hadn’t used those names from the start.

What mattered was that the first Heavenly Demon never conquered the world. He and his followers settled instead in a desert on the western frontier.

Over nearly a thousand years, they sometimes turned their blades on one another. After several divisions and reunifications, they brought about the Great Faction War, a calamity of enormous scale.

Throughout those centuries, the scorching desert remained under the absolute rule of Fiends. It became the Land of Ruin, a place even the martial world of the Central Plains couldn’t hope to challenge.

And so it remained, even now that a new shadow called Dark Heaven had fallen across it.

But…

“Something unprecedented is happening in *that* Xinjiang?”

A long silence had followed my brief account. Namho’s question finally broke it, and I nodded.

“That’s what I heard.”

“Where did the information come from?”

“Where do you think?”

I pointed upward. Namho let out a weary breath.

“The Murim Alliance… No, the Hidden Shadow Pavilion.”

“I heard they lost more than thirty elite agents. After their first and last messenger eagle, all contact was lost.”

“Damn it. Then there’s no room for doubt. How many enemies are we looking at?”

“That…”

Even I couldn’t bring myself to answer right away. Namho groaned at the look on my face.

“I see. The Hundred Thousand Demonic Disciples all over again.”

“Maybe more than that. At least, that’s what the information I received suggests.”

More important, these enemies would be far stronger and more terrifying than the Demonic Cult’s followers had been fifty years ago.

The desert no longer belonged to the Demonic Cult. It belonged to Dark Heaven.

They weren’t merely the old cult’s successors under a new name. They were new followers ruled by something incomparably more powerful and dangerous.

*Could that be why Logout was suddenly blocked?*

The thought crossed my mind, but I shook my head.

No. That wasn’t it.

The Quest hadn’t appeared yet, because I hadn’t made my choice.

Now was the moment to make it.

“From this moment on, we’re heading west.”

Ding.

An alert sounded, announcing a new Quest.
